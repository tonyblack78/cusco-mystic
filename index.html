Why we started here

A valley that has fed and healed for 500 generations

The Sacred Valley of the Inca — Cusco, Peru — sits at 9,000 feet between glacier peaks, terraced farmland, and a river the Inca called the Willkamayu, the "Sacred River." Long before it was a tourist route to Machu Picchu, it was a working landscape: salt pans cut into a hillside, corn bred over centuries for altitude and flavor, and farming communities who kept their methods largely unchanged.

Cusco Mystic exists to bring three of that valley's oldest provisions to the U.S. honestly — naming where they're from, who makes them, and what we owe back to that place for letting us carry them out.

"Water that falls from the glacier, salt that rises from the mountain."

The Collection

Three provisions, one valley

Each product is tied to a specific place and a specific set of hands. Here's what each one is, plainly.

01 — Salt

Maras Pink Salt

Hand-harvested from the ancient salt terraces of Maras, where a subterranean spring has carried mineral-rich water down the hillside since pre-Inca times. Evaporated in shallow pools and raked by hand, same as it's been done for centuries. Unrefined, additive-free, naturally pink from trace minerals.

02 — Water

Andean Mineral Water

Pure Andean spring water, bottled and finished with a light top layer of Maras salt — the same trace minerals (sodium, magnesium, potassium, calcium) that give the salt its color, in water form. A crisp, naturally mineralized alternative to flat spring water.

Cusco Cornbucha

A corn-based fermented beverage inspired by chicha de jora — the ceremonial corn drink of Andean communities — reimagined as a lighter, kombucha-style ferment. Purple and white Andean corn, brought from the Sacred Valley, fermented in Los Angeles. Tart, lightly effervescent, distinctly corn-forward. Launching soon.

The Land — Maras, Sacred Valley

13,000 pools, carved by hand into a single hillside

Above the town of Maras, a saline spring has run down the mountainside since well before the Inca. Local families — the same families, generation to generation — channel that water into thousands of shallow terraced pools. The sun and wind do the rest, leaving mineral-rich salt crystals to be raked out by hand.

We buy directly from cooperative pool-owners in Maras, at prices set with them, not against them.

The People — Quechua Communities

Textiles, farming, and ceremony that pre-date the Inca empire

The Sacred Valley is home to Quechua-speaking communities whose weaving, farming calendars, and ceremonial life — offerings to the Apus, the mountain spirits, and to Pachamama, the earth — remain central to daily life. Our corn is grown by small family plots in these communities; our salt, by the pool-owning families of Maras.

We work with local partners on the ground rather than through a single export broker, so more of what we pay reaches the people doing the work.
